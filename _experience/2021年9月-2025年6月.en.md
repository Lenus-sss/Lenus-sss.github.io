# September 2021–June 2025

##### “In 2021, I was 18 years old and, like most of my classmates, wanted to study computer science.”

Zhang Xuefeng was very popular at the time, and everyone believed that civil engineering at a Project 985 university was worse than computer science at an ordinary second-tier university. As a result, the admission scores for computer science were extremely high, and I was reassigned to the physics department.

I was quite happy, because physics was my favorite subject.

But over those four years, my grades were unremarkable, and I was not proactive enough. Hardly any professors paid attention to me, so I never had the opportunity to do research.

Physics had abandoned me, I thought.

I often holed up in my dorm playing single-player games and refused to go to class. Fortunately, my roommates were very kind: they let me copy their homework, answered roll call for me, and even brought me a bowl of chicken-leg rice after class.

By my sophomore year, I could no longer resist teaching myself programming.

Even more fortunately, I met a few naïve classmates who actually wanted to develop a AAA game to save China’s domestic gaming industry—despite knowing nothing about programming, art, game engines, or project planning, and having no experience whatsoever. Black Myth had not yet been released at the time. I was astonished and joined them immediately. We used Unity.

Naturally, it failed. We entered our game demo in the Internet+ competition but did not make it past the initial screening. The team lost heart and stopped development.

They say a successful game producer must develop at least ten failed games. To amuse myself, I made a galgame and a simple gunfight game demo.

AI was not around yet, so development was extremely difficult. If only I had been born two years later.

Studying physics was very demanding. If you searched for assignments from our physics department online, you usually could not find anything, and even when you did, the answers were often complete nonsense. I found learning programming much easier than studying physics because the internet was full of freely shared courses and demos.

For a while, I happily roamed the internet.

I only had a thin-and-light laptop, and its battery drained quickly whenever it was unplugged. Every morning, I had to rush to the library to claim a seat with a power outlet, go to class, then return to play around with programming. At noon, I ate boiled fish at the cafeteria. In the afternoon, I studied the major courses I liked for a while, and in the evening, I went off campus for Yuanji Yun Dumplings.

My only annoyance was that whenever I ran Unity, my laptop howled like a beast, drawing disgusted looks from the students around me and leaving me rather embarrassed.

So in my senior year, I started looking for an internship. I desperately needed a better machine.

I made what I believed was the right decision.

I turned down ByteDance and joined a small software company closer to my university. Their equipment was fairly ordinary, but it was good enough. The workload was light, and once I finished my tasks, I could do whatever I wanted. It was practically like having access to a free internet café, and far more comfortable than university. There, I completed my studies while filling the gaps in my computer science fundamentals in a relaxed environment. When I had nothing else to do, I could even compete in LeetCode contests. The HR manager at the time was a true foodie, and the company’s afternoon tea was delicious.

One day, I noticed that all my colleagues were using ChatGPT. I tried it myself and was completely blown away.

I suddenly felt that I had entered a field on the verge of collapse. If large language models continued developing like this, the day programmers lost their jobs would come sooner or later. I immediately deployed a Qwen model locally and began fine-tuning it with LoRA. Before I could fully figure out AI, however, I graduated.

Because I was immersed in my own research, I missed both the autumn and spring recruitment seasons.

It was not until my academic advisor came to me and asked me to sign a fake employment contract—otherwise, I would hurt the department’s employment rate—that I realized how bad things had become.

I frantically submitted résumés for AI roles through the campus recruitment websites of major companies, but the applications went nowhere. Every one of them remained stuck in the candidate pool.

About two weeks later, I changed direction and started applying for Unreal Engine and Unity game-development positions on general recruitment websites. To my surprise, everything went extremely smoothly, and I quickly received several offers. The technical interviewer from Tencent was exceptionally capable and had a calm, composed presence. Half a month later, I boarded a plane and arrived at Tencent.

By then, it was already 2025.
